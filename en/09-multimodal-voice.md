## MM-001. Tomorrow a text-only bot must also ingest a user screenshot and a call recording. What do you turn each signal into first, and how does the pipeline stay runnable when one modality is missing?

**Answer:** The screenshot goes through a vision encoder into patch or region vectors; the recording goes through an acoustic front-end into frame- or segment-level features; existing text still goes through a tokenizer. All three must be projected into the same width the language model can read (or the same family of vision/audio tokens). Projection is alignment, not concatenating raw pixels and waveforms into one matrix. A missing image or missing audio is a first-class input: emit a learned placeholder from that tower, or write no tokens for that modality at all—do not feed a zero vector that pretends the model “looked.” Keep timestamps on the recording and spatial coordinates on the screenshot; without that metadata you cannot later answer “what was said at second 12 / the price in the red box.”

**Deep dive:** The data path is raw bytes → modality-specific preprocessing (resize or tile the image to the encoder’s input size; resample audio to e.g. 16 kHz and compute log-mel with a window of about 25 ms and a hop of 10–20 ms) → a vision backbone (ViT/CNN) and an audio backbone (Conformer/Transformer-class) → a linear or shallow MLP into language width D (often about 1024–4096, depending on the LLM) → concatenation with text tokens into one sequence. A ~336-wide image can yield about 500–600 visual tokens; 10 s of audio at roughly one token per 40 ms is about 250 tokens. Putting both into an 8k window immediately crowds out multi-turn history, so long recordings are usually pooled by segment or transcribed first, keeping acoustic tokens only for critical spans. Spatial grounding needs the patch grid or detection-box ids; temporal alignment needs frame indices—one global summary vector is not enough. Sensor readings and order tables should not be forced into the vision tower; use a structured encoder or serialize them as text. When image and speech disagree (the photo shows damage, the user says “it’s fine”), run a consistency check after generation and require a pointer to a region or timestamp; do not average the two feature streams away during encoding.

**Follow-ups:** Whether each modality gets its own projection or a shared one, and how gradients flow when a modality is absent; whether a 60-second recording should be ASR’d to text before the LLM, or whether some acoustic tokens must stay to preserve tone and overlap; when the image and the recording contradict, whether you trust OCR, the transcript, or a forced clarification.

**Extended questions:**
- If the three modalities differ in vector norm by an order of magnitude, do you length-normalize after projection, and how does skipping that pollute attention?
- The user sent only an image, but the system prompt asks what was said on the call—can a missing-modality placeholder be read as fake evidence that the user stayed silent?
- How would you build a set that is answerable from one modality and deliberately contradictory with two, to prove the model is not quietly dropping the weaker stream?

**Reasoning:** The goal is to use image and audio in the same turn; the constraints are missing modalities, traceability to a region or timestamp, and a finite context. Encode in separate towers, project, and let interaction happen only after the language side reads those tokens; decide transcription versus kept frames from the token budget. Check that missing-modality cases still answer, that conflict cases get flagged, and that vision/audio token counts do not evict dialogue history.

## MM-002. You have three lines—image-to-copy retrieval, visual QA on spec sheets, and text-to-promo-image. Which of dual-encoder, vision-encoder-plus-LLM, and a diffusion backbone do you bind to which line, and where does forcing one backbone onto all three break first?

**Answer:** Bind retrieval to a dual encoder: image and text towers encode independently and meet only at similarity. Vectors can be precomputed and indexed with ANN; online cost is one encode plus neighbors. Bind visual QA to a vision encoder + projection + autoregressive LLM: you need cross-column reasoning and sentence assembly, so the language side must reread visual tokens. Bind image generation to conditional diffusion (or a similar generative backbone): the objective is a latent/pixel trajectory, not next token on a vocabulary. The losses, output interfaces, and latency shapes differ. Using the mean of LLM hidden states as a catalog index makes vectors drift with the prompt and kills precompute. Forcing a dual encoder to decode will not write an answer that needs calculation. Using diffusion as retrieval yields “looks like” instead of “this SKU.”

**Deep dive:** Dual encoders interact late and amortize compute offline—the home of a million-item catalog. The weakness is that image and text meet through one vector; compositional constraints (“red shoe and a perforated sole”) stay weak and need a cross-encoder rerank. A VLM writes hundreds of visual tokens into context for multi-step reasoning, which fits “what is the wattage in column three of this product page”; the cost is prefilling those tokens on every question, and the cache key must include the image version, unlike a text-only session. Diffusion iteratively denoises in a VAE latent, with text injected by cross-attention; there is no retrieval primary key, only a generation trajectory. A unified-token Transformer can serve multi-task understanding but still cannot emit a picture unless you attach a generative head. The first hard-sharing failure is usually the interface: retrieval wants a stable, indexable vector; generation wants a stochastic trajectory; QA wants a conditional language distribution. One backbone cannot be all three heads. A shallow vision front-end may be shared; the loss must not.

**Follow-ups:** Whether image-to-copy retrieval should add a light cross-encoder after the dual towers; whether QA should take OCR text as a second condition instead of relying only on visual tokens; whether generation’s text condition should come from the contrastive text tower or from an LLM-rewritten prompt.

**Extended questions:**
- Putting patches and words in one unified Transformer sequence, versus freezing the vision tower and training only a projector—what do the two choices demand in data volume and training stability?
- If the retrieval tower and the VLM vision tower share weights, which drops first, recall or fine-grained localization?
- When a promo image must contain a specified SKU photo, should the condition attach to diffusion or should you retrieve-then-composite, and why can you not just turn the retrieval tower into a generator?

**Reasoning:** Choose the interface from the output type (neighbor id, text answer, pixels), then choose a backbone that can be precomputed versus one that must be autoregressive or iterative. Sharing is allowed only at shallow visual features. Accept each line on its own headline metric—Recall@K, QA accuracy, condition adherence—and on whether misuse of the wrong backbone is blocked.

## MM-003. Dermatology triage must read a lesion photo and the patient’s own words. If you concatenate pixels and word vectors at the front of the model versus scoring two specialist models and then weighting, which path dies first when the photo is missing?

**Answer:** Early fusion concatenates unaligned visual and text features in the front of the encoder; a missing photo leaves a hole or a zero pad, and without modality-dropout training that path breaks first. Late fusion lets a skin vision model and a chief-complaint text model emit disease scores independently and then weights them; with no image it can still score from the text expert, but it cannot see fine conflicts such as “patient wrote dry, the photo weeps.” When missing modalities are common, late fusion is the runnable floor; add cross-attention or a consistency head only when both paths are present.

**Deep dive:** Early-fusion data path: image patches and text tokens enter the same self-attention, or features are concatenated in the first layers. It can learn whether the word “blister” matches highlights and edges in that region; the cost is paired training, coupled sequence lengths, and compute that grows with the concatenated sequence. Missing-modality handling is random drop at train time or a learned empty-image token; if production suddenly sends only a complaint, that placeholder never saw real edge statistics and scores bias or collapse. Late-fusion data path: a vision tower emits skin-disease superclass logits, a text tower classifies the complaint, then logistic regression, learned weights, or a rule (raise vision weight when a photo exists) merges them. You can scale and degrade each expert independently—robust to missingness—but the two paths meet only at the decision, so they cannot ground pixel-level evidence. Hybrid: encode in separate towers, then a few cross-attention layers (mid fusion), and explicitly train text-only / image-only / both. In clinic, a large share of users type first and attach a photo later, or the photo is too blurry to use, so “both modalities required” cannot be the default. Score conflicts separately: when the two experts disagree, emit uncertain and hand off, rather than hard-wiring “always trust the photo” or “always trust the complaint.” Early fusion is not “newer, therefore better”—it only interacts earlier and is more brittle to missingness.

**Follow-ups:** If training already dropped the image 30% of the time, is early fusion enough as the only path; one expert at 0.9 and the other at 0.2—emit the high-score disease or force uncertain; the patient later uploads someone else’s web skin photo—how does the late-fusion vision expert get pulled off course, and do you add a source or quality gate.

**Extended questions:**
- For image-missing, text-missing, and both-present inputs, which of early, late, and mid fusion can still emit a calibrated score.
- Should “complaint vs lesion visual contradiction” be its own head before fusion, or do you trust early self-attention to notice it?
- How do you use still-triageable-without-photo rate, image-text conflict recall, and wrong-disease rate to justify the fusion point, instead of accuracy only when both modalities are present?

**Reasoning:** The constraint is that clinic modalities are often incomplete, and a wrong diagnosis costs more than skipping one cross-attention. Guarantee degrade-without-photo first, then add interaction to catch conflicts when both paths exist. Accept on missing-modality and conflict slices, not on “earlier concat is more advanced.”

## MM-004. Photo search must rank two shots of the same cat as neighbors even when the pixels differ, so you cannot compare raw images. What kind of vector should each photo become, and why must the old index be rebuilt after you swap the encoder?

**Answer:** An encoder trained on a large image collection (CNN, ViT, or the image tower of an image–text contrastive model) maps each photo to a fixed-length vector whose training objective makes semantically close images have a large dot product or cosine—not a small pixel L2. Two cat photos can differ completely in edges and background and should still land nearby; a cat and a car should not. Length-normalize, then use cosine or inner product inside an ANN index. Swapping the encoder, the preprocessing, or the pooling layer changes the coordinate system: a “1.0” in the old space is not the same direction as in the new one. Mixing them silently shuffles neighbors. Re-embed the corpus under the pair (model version, preprocessing) and rebuild the index.

**Deep dive:** Pixels are an H×W×3 observation; shift, crop, and exposure explode per-pixel distance while semantics stay put. The network builds edges → textures → parts → whole-image meaning; you typically take the last-layer CLS or global average pool, often about 512–1024 dimensions (backbone-dependent). The objective defines “near”: closed-set classification leans toward object class, self-supervised contrast toward transform invariance, image–text contrast toward scenes a phrase can name. This question only requires image-to-image comparability; do not treat text-to-image search as the same interface. A million 768-d float32 vectors are on the order of 3 GB raw; production quantizes and builds HNSW or IVF-style ANN. Boundary: watermarks, shared borders, and screenshot chrome become shortcuts—neighbors are really “the same template.” A tiny subject in the corner gets washed out by global pooling; detect then embed. Measure stability with Recall@K after flip, crop, and JPEG, not on clean studio shots only. If 10% of the index still holds old-model vectors, neighbors look irreproducibly near-then-far.

**Follow-ups:** How recall and storage each move when you go from 256-d to 1024-d, and whether there is a plateau; whether a resolution-only change with the same weights still requires a rebuild; why exact pairwise compare is impossible at a million items, and how you accept ANN recall versus latency.

**Extended questions:**
- A single global vector collapses “cat on a sofa” into one point—when do you switch to multi-vector or region vectors so subject and background can separate?
- How would you build positives (“same SKU, different shoot”) and negatives (“different SKU, same studio backdrop”) to catch background shortcuts?
- If the online reader still sees 10% old-model leftovers, what does the neighbor list look like, and how does a version field refuse mixed reads?

**Reasoning:** The goal is semantic neighbors, not pixel neighbors, so the representation must come from an encoder whose objective defines the space, scored with normalized cosine. When the axes change, the index key must carry the model version. Accept on Recall@K under transforms, and on a hard reject of the old index after a swap.

## MM-005. Asked “how many cups are in the picture,” the model says 2, but there are 4 half-occluded cups—how do you tell language-prior guessing from actually counting in the image, and which traps should the eval set plant on purpose?

**Answer:** Visual question answering needs the answer to rest on image evidence, not on word statistics in the question. “Cup” often co-occurs with “two” in text, so a model biased toward 2 will not look at occlusions. To separate the two behaviors, eval must make a question-only baseline fail: change the image and keep the question, or put a misleading noun in the question. In production, require the regions used for the count (boxes or points); a box that does not match an object is wrong even if the number is luckily right.

**Deep dive:** Language prior is the classic VQA leak: training data answers “what color is the banana” with yellow so often that the model answers yellow from the question. Counting, existence, and spatial relations are worse: occlusion, truncation, and reflections drop detections, and the model fills in a “usual” number. A trap set includes at least: (1) the same question on different images with gold 2 / 4 / 0; (2) the question says “cups” but the image shows bowls, to see if the noun hijacks the answer; (3) “what color is the one on the left” when the left side is empty; (4) reading tasks where the printed number disagrees with the question’s hint. Metrics: closed counts use accuracy; open descriptions are scored separately; localization matches boxes to instances and a missed instance is a miss. In production it is stabler to detect or segment candidates first and let the language model count or compare on that set—also easier to debug visually. High-resolution crops matter for small objects; shrink the whole image to a short side around 224–336 and the back-row cups disappear. Do not argue “the model is large, therefore it looks.” Run a control that hides the image and leaves the question: if accuracy barely drops, the system is not doing VQA.

**Follow-ups:** Should counting be “detect then count boxes” or a direct number from the model, and how do the error shapes differ; what new prior does multi-image comparison (“which photo has more cups”) introduce; if the user asks a subjective question (“does this look good”), do you still require a box.

**Extended questions:**
- Design a hide-the-image control; what result would convince you the system used pixels?
- For instances occluded more than halfway, should the model count them, mark uncertainty, or refuse to count?
- Why can VQA, image captioning, and image–text retrieval not share one score?

**Reasoning:** The goal is that the answer moves when pixels move, not when question unigrams move. The constraint is a strong language prior and occlusion that breaks detection. Separate guessing from looking with a control experiment and mandatory evidence boxes, and count visible instances. Accept on trap-set accuracy and on whether hiding the image collapses the score—a collapse is a healthy sign.

## MM-006. With only eight thousand product-image instruction pairs and a frozen vision encoder, should fine-tuning hit the projector or language-side LoRA? How do you stop the model from reciting catalog copy without looking at the image?

**Answer:** At about eight thousand pairs, train the vision-to-language projector (a linear layer or shallow MLP) first, then adapt the language side with low-rank adapters; leave the vision encoder frozen. The projector aligns in-domain appearance to the LLM token space; LoRA teaches the “look at the image, then fill slots” answer format. The way to block copy-reciting is not more product manuals. Pair the same text with different images so the answer must change: strip catalog fields that already contain the answer, and evaluate with image-swap, question-fixed.

**Deep dive:** A typical VLM path is ViT visual tokens → project into the LLM hidden space → concatenate with the question → autoregressive answer. Eight thousand pairs are nowhere near enough to update a vision stack with hundreds of millions of weights: even if the encoder learning rate is an order of magnitude below the language side, unfreezing it often washes out generic edges, materials, and glyph strokes and overfits shop backgrounds. The projector is usually millions to tens of millions of parameters; a learning rate on the order of 1e-4 and one to three epochs can align in-domain color and pack outlines. Language-side LoRA at rank 8–64, with a learning rate another half to one notch lower, only writes incremental attention and MLP updates. If the job is mainly a vocabulary shift (“object” → “SKU attributes”), the projector alone can be enough; structured JSON and refuse-on-blurry-image behavior usually need the language adapter. Reciting happens when the user turn or system prompt already says “blue, two-piece set”: cross-entropy drops on the text side and gradients on visual tokens look like noise. Construction: the same product blurb with color/count variant photos, labels only for attributes visible in the pixels; add “if text and image disagree, say so” pairs. Acceptance needs a “fixed question, swapped image, answer must flip” slice, not fluency of open captions. Boundary: if the projector cannot read tiny hangtags, add high-resolution tiles or an OCR tool rather than unfreezing the tower; fields a tool can extract should not be memorized into weights.

**Follow-ups:** The projector already aligns but the model still will not fill fields in your schema order—add LoRA or constrain decoding; two thousand of the eight thousand pairs reuse one hero image with different wording, how much visual diversity is left; if you unfreeze the last two vision blocks at a 10× lower learning rate, which freeze-vs-unfreeze ablations decide whether that is worth it.

**Extended questions:**
- Design an image-swap eval: list at least three attribute classes that must flip (color, count, defect location).
- If full price-tag OCR still leaks into the training prompt, what shortcut will LoRA learn, and where do you cut it in the data pipeline?
- How do you use conflict-set accuracy, image-swap flip rate, and drop on generic VQA together to show you froze the right layers instead of trading general vision for local memorization?

**Reasoning:** The goal is in-domain visual answering; the constraints are a small set and a locked vision tower. Freeze the large, already-general module; train only the alignment interface and the output format. Define success as “the answer must move when the image moves” so a text shortcut cannot turn fine-tuning into manual memorization.

## MM-007. Product categories grow every week and you refuse to retrain a classification head each time. After contrastive learning aligns images and text, what is zero-shot classification actually comparing?

**Answer:** CLIP-style dual encoders train on large image–text pairs: matched image and text vectors are pulled together, other in-batch pairings are pushed apart, so short phrases and pictures share one metric space. Zero-shot classification does not grow a new class head. It encodes one prompt phrase per class, takes cosine with the image vector, and picks the nearest phrase as the label. A new category is a new line in the prompt table—no visual-tower retrain. That tracks an open vocabulary better than “collect a few thousand images per class and train a linear head,” but it is prompt-sensitive and still weak on fine attributes and counting.

**Deep dive:** One training step (structural sketch): a batch of N images and N captions, two towers emit N normalized vectors, an N×N similarity matrix, the diagonal is the positive, and a symmetric image→text / text→image cross-entropy updates both towers; a temperature stretches the gap. Inference no longer needs that training matrix—only K dot products of one image against K class texts, and K from tens to tens of thousands is still far cheaper than retraining a head. The design stuck as a plug-in aligner: text-to-image and image-to-text retrieval, conditioning or filtering for generators, visual initialization for VLMs. The lasting value is the open-vocabulary interface, not one season’s classification score. Boundary: if pairs skew toward e-commerce photos, medical slices and pure UI screenshots shift; attributes like “red” or “round” get dominated by object words; dirty captions write shortcuts into the space. In production you usually freeze both towers, iterate prompt templates, and regress on a small fixed category set rather than default-finetuning the towers.

**Follow-ups:** Where the zero-shot gap usually appears between a two- or three-character Chinese class name and a full “a photo of …” template; how contrastive learning collapses when the batch is too small and negatives are too easy; if retrieval uses this space but image generation uses another text encoder, which space wins when they disagree.

**Extended questions:**
- Two near-synonym prompts compete for the same image in an open taxonomy—do you average multiple templates or add a small reranker?
- If the training pairs contain a lot of clickbait titles, how do you down-weight in the loss or the filter so the space does not follow ad copy?
- Why is this space a fit for retrieval and zero-shot classification, yet usually the wrong thing to use as a pixel-level segmentation head?

**Reasoning:** The constraint is an open, weekly-growing taxonomy and no weekly head retrain. Rewrite classification as nearest neighbor between an image vector and class-name text vectors; train with in-batch contrast to align the towers. Accept on whether new categories work zero-shot, on prompt-template sensitivity, and on the gap versus a closed-set linear head on old categories—not on one blended accuracy.

## MM-008. A user photographs a shoe sole to find the matching product page—where does recall break if image and text are not in one space, what do dual-tower alignment and late rerank each fix, and why do you still need OCR when the SKU copy is only a few characters?

**Answer:** Cross-modal search needs a distance that is meaningful between the query modality and the indexed modality. A dual tower trained with contrastive pairing puts a sole photo near copy like “rubber outsole, herringbone tread,” so nearest-neighbor search can hit a product page from a photo. If image and text use encoders that were never aligned, distance is undefined and recall dies at “you cannot compare.” The towers do coarse recall; color, size, and anti-counterfeit texture need cropped local features and a cross reranker. A sole photo rarely has a readable brand, but the box and hangtag do: OCR’d SKUs should be promoted through an inverted index, because a short SKU is unstable inside a sentence vector.

**Deep dive:** The training target is a contrastive loss on (image, text) pairs with in-batch negatives; at inference the towers run separately—gallery vectors offline, query through the image tower or the text tower only. The space must come from one training run, one temperature, and one normalization; mixing two vector versions is a change of coordinates. Dirty product data: studio shots on white and user photos are far apart, so a space trained only on studio soles drifts toward “texture close-up” instead of the SKU. Mitigate by indexing a whole-image vector, a subject crop (shoe, sole), and a sparse index on OCR or title. Rerank the top-50 with a heavier image–text matcher or attribute checks (color, closure); do not expect a few-hundred-dimensional global vector to separate two nearly identical shoes. Eval must be cross-modal slices: text→image, image→text, image→image; unimodal classification accuracy does not speak to this. After a tower change, every old vector is invalid and the index is rebuilt. Illustrative scale: 100k SKUs × 5 images is 500k vectors, usually fine for a graph index; the hard cases are long-tail styles with one image and copy that is only a part number.

**Follow-ups:** If the user photographs the box and the gallery has only vamp shots, what neighbors does the dual tower return, and should you index pack shots; when copy is only a SKU, contrastive learning has almost no semantics to align—how do you build training pairs; do image→image and text→image share the same image vector.

**Extended questions:**
- Why does a new image-tower version force a full reindex, and can you get by with a linear map on old vectors?
- If you use only an open-world image–text space and skip product fine-tuning, on which categories do competitors systematically rank first?
- Should cross-modal recall report Recall@K only, or also attribute accuracy for “same model, different color”?

**Reasoning:** The goal is that a photo hits the right product page. The constraint is that an unaligned space is not comparable, short SKUs are semantically weak, and fine appearance exceeds a global vector. Use the dual tower for comparable coarse recall, promote SKUs with OCR, and rerank for fine differences. Accept on same-item Recall@K from photos and on whether lookalike competitors are pushed down.

## MM-009. A 4K product screenshot has a price only a few dozen pixels tall, but the VLM replies that it cannot read it. Along the path from pixels to language tokens, where is that detail most likely discarded?

**Answer:** A vision-language model does not hand raw pixels to the LLM. It resizes or tiles → a vision encoder emits patch features → a connector (MLP, Q-Former, or a perceiver-style resampler) compresses them into fewer visual tokens → those tokens are concatenated with the prompt. On a 4K long screenshot the price is often only tens of pixels tall; the first resize of the whole page to e.g. 336² or 448² already smears the digits into texture. Tiling still drops a decimal that straddles two tiles if overlap is thin or each tile has a token cap. OCR or detection boxes are a side path for small text, not the default—without them the model can only guess the price from pooled semantic features.

**Deep dive:** Take a typical ViT: the input is cut into P×P patches (e.g. 14×14). A 336-wide image is about 24×24 = 576 patches, each seeing only its own cell. If the price is already below stroke width after resize, the encoder never had the high frequencies. The connector often collapses hundreds of patches into a few dozen to a couple of hundred language-side tokens—a second lossy step: category and dominant color survive, tiny digits die first. The high-resolution pattern is to run the encoder on a grid of tiles (2×2 or more) plus one downsampled full image for layout; visual token count scales roughly with the number of tiles, so prefill gets more expensive. For “must read the glyphs” requests, a more stable route is: tile the long image by screenful → inject OCR as text tokens → keep visual tokens for icons, color, and column structure. Boundary: icon meaning, relative position, and whether a red cross is present still need the vision tower; you cannot OCR everything. “What category is this?” and “the discounted price on row three” should not share one resolution.

**Follow-ups:** Whether tiling without OCR is enough for a handwritten scanned price; what criterion you use to compress 576 patches to 64 tokens; whether coarse and fine questions on the same image should route to different vision front-ends.

**Extended questions:**
- After tiling, left- and right-column prices get swapped—how do you attach region ids or coordinates to visual tokens so the language model does not guess columns from reading order alone?
- If the connector was trained to “summarize the whole image,” does fine-grained localization break first as misaligned attention maps or as the language side inventing digits?
- How would you use a small-digit set and a large-object set to prove the bottleneck is geometric compression, not decoding temperature?

**Reasoning:** A price is a high-frequency, small target, so the failure should sit in geometric compression before the LLM, not in sampling noise. Mark the information bottlenecks at resize → patch → resample, then choose tiling, OCR, or per-question resolution routing. Accept on small-text read accuracy and visual-token / prefill cost, not on whether a free-form caption sounds fluent.

## MM-010. The invoice total sits next to a stamp in the bottom-right, and reading-order OCR stuffs the tax into the total field—how do layout coordinates, table structure, and field linking enter the model so you extract by box instead of concatenating lines?

**Answer:** Reading order is not field semantics: totals, tax, and account numbers often sit in a footer or beside a stamp, and concatenating lines sucks a nearby digit into the wrong slot. Document understanding keeps `(text, x, y, w, h, page)` on every text block, keeps a cell grid on tables, and predicts links from a label box to a value box, instead of treating page OCR as left-to-right prose. Visual cues (rules, shading, stamps) and language (“Amount Due,” the local equivalent of “total with tax”) must be used together; drop either side and multi-column plus mixed-script layouts fail.

**Deep dive:** Pipeline: scan or PDF → layout analysis (paragraphs, tables, stamps, handwriting) → text recognition → structure. Prefer the native PDF text layer’s coordinates when it exists; OCR only scans; when both appear, unify the coordinate system (points or pixels, record DPI). Do not linearize a table into “the second number on row 3” and drop the grid: wrapped headers and merged cells must keep row/column spans. Field extraction is a linking problem on the page: label left / value right, label above / value below, or a distant “total” pointing at large type in the bottom-right. Model inputs can be tokens plus 2D positional encodings, or a rendered page that must emit JSON plus a box per field. Write validation rules before trusting the model: total not less than tax, currency glyph matches the column, account checksum; failures go to a human at low confidence—do not post a “number-shaped string” straight into the ledger. Boundaries: stamps over glyphs, faded thermal paper, magazine multi-column, and tables that cross a page all break pure reading order. Versus “give the whole page to a general vision-language model,” explicit layout is stabler for field-level audit and batch invoices; a general model fits the long tail of wild templates and shifting schemas.

**Follow-ups:** If the text layer and OCR disagree on the same field, who wins; if a table’s header is on the previous page, how do you build the link features across the page break; if a handwritten amount and a printed amount both exist, which one is canonical.

**Extended questions:**
- Why does “OCR the page, then let a language model fill JSON” systematically put right-column numbers into left-column fields on a two-column invoice?
- Should field-link eval report field F1 only or also box IoU, and what does F1-only hide?
- On a sudden template change, do you adapt with a few samples, or do you add validation rules first to stop bad extractions?

**Reasoning:** The goal is the right number in the business field, pointed at by a box. The constraint is that reading order is not layout semantics, and nearby digits steal slots. Treat coordinates and the table grid as first-class input, predict links, and validate with rules. Accept on field-level accuracy and on whether the box sits on the correct amount, not on whole-page transcription overlap.

## MM-011. The user asks "what did they change right before clicking Settings," but the inverted index only returns result frames that contain the word Settings. How do you turn cursor trails or window-title changes into predecessor edges between shots, instead of stuffing the surrounding thirty seconds into a vision model and hoping it pages through them?

**Answer:** The question asks for the predecessor of the Settings click, not for frames that contain the word Settings. Record window-title changes, cursor dwells, and clicks as event nodes, then walk a predecessor edge backward from the "entered Settings" node to the most recent value-changing event (toggle, dropdown, committed field). Retrieval follows that edge and sends only the predecessor's keyframe and widget crop to the vision model. A raw ±30 s pixel window is expensive and pulls attention back onto the result page's "Settings" text.

**Deep dive:** An inverted index over OCR or transcript is a bag of words: the Settings title, menu items, and a status chip "settings saved" are all high-weight hits, and they are the result state. Causes need a separate event graph. Nodes look like `(t, type, payload)` with `type` in `title_change`, `click`, `text_commit`, `toggle`, `focus`. Title is cheap: the accessibility tree, or a title-bar OCR crop about every 0.5–1 s, orders of magnitude cheaper than a full-frame VLM. Cursor samples `(x,y,t)`; a press plus a short dwell is a click, snapped to the widget box at that time. Two edge types: `precedes`—on interaction time (idle removed), no other value change sits between the edit and the later "title became Settings / gear click"; `cursor_from_to`—the pointer left widget A and then pointed at the Settings entry. ## MM-019. Walking from a canvas of Gaussian noise to a 512×512 image, what are the forward noising and reverse denoising processes each optimizing, and why do many systems run those steps in latent space rather than on the pixel grid?

**Answer:** The training-time forward process adds noise to a clean image on a schedule until it is approximately Gaussian. The network learns the reverse: given a timestep and optional text, predict the noise or the clean image so you can iterate back from pure noise. At generation there is no original image—only noise, updated once per step for as many steps as the sampler specifies (illustrative: a dozen to tens or hundreds of steps, sampler-dependent, not a product spec). Latent diffusion first compresses pixels with a VAE-class model to a smaller feature map and denoises there. Spatial side length often shrinks by about 8× (treat the factor as structural, actual VAE-dependent), so attention and convolution visit an order of magnitude fewer locations; multi-step sampling then fits in servable memory and latency.

**Deep dive:** In pixel space a 512×512×3 state is large; every step runs conv or attention at full resolution. Latent space keeps similar semantics on a coarser grid with more channels, so the denoiser can run repeatedly on one GPU. Text is encoded as a sequence and injected into the denoiser via cross-attention. Classifier-free guidance combines a conditional and an unconditional prediction with a scale: too high saturates, too low ignores the prompt—this is a sampling-time control, not a second content model. DDIM and ODE samplers change the step–quality curve; content still comes from the denoiser. Boundary: latent space is capped by VAE reconstruction—small glyphs and sharp edges blur first, and extra denoising steps alone will not repair that. Too few steps break structure; turning guidance up does not replace missing steps. Do not confuse this with autoregressive image-token models: diffusion iteratively refines a whole-image state, it does not write pixels in raster order from the top-left.

**Extended questions:**
- Why does training sample timesteps (uniformly or by a schedule) instead of teaching only the last few denoising steps?
- If the condition changes from a short phrase to a spatial mask or an edge map, is cross-attention or channel concatenation the better injection point?
- How do you use reconstruction error to split “the VAE dropped the strokes” from “the denoiser missed the prompt”?

**Reasoning:** Define generation as a reversible noise chain: the forward process supplies training pairs, the reverse defines sampling. Compute moves the state from pixels into latents; text is only a per-step condition. - If the background task finishes while the agent is answering something else, do you interrupt, wait for a sentence end, or defer to the next turn?
- Can a timeout retry double-book, and which layer owns the idempotency key?

**Reasoning:** The goal is a live line and no lying about results. The constraint is that write tools have side effects, latency is uncontrolled, and the model will fill in fields it has not seen. Turn the tool into an observable background task, lock the copy behind an incomplete status, then backstop with timeout and idempotency. Accept on longest silence, timeout rate, fake “you’re booked” incidents, and task completion.

## MM-031. The silence window has not declared the user’s turn over, but you already started the LLM on the current transcript. After end-of-turn is confirmed, how do you compare inputs to decide “play the buffer” versus “discard and rerun,” so first-token time hides inside the wait?

**Answer:** The ordinary path is serial: wait for endpointing, then request the LLM, then TTS. Preemptive generation (also called speculative generation in voice agents; not the same as speculative decoding, where a small model drafts tokens for a large model) sends the current stable transcript to the LLM before end-of-turn is confirmed. The completion goes to a buffer and is never played. After endpointing, compare “the text plus dialogue state and tool list at request time” with “the final transcript and state for this turn.” On a match, hand the buffer to TTS immediately—TTFT has been overlapped with silence you were going to wait anyway. On a mismatch, cancel the in-flight request, drop the buffer, and rerun on the final text. The user never hears a wrong half sentence; you pay the discarded tokens.

**Deep dive:** Sketch: a 500 ms silence window and 400 ms TTFT. Serial needs 900 ms before TTS can start; preemption folds 400 ms into the 500 ms, saving about one TTFT on a hit, capped by the silence window itself. The user says “a table for two…” and pauses; the buffer already holds “what time?”; they continue “…at 8 p.m., by the window.” The input changed: cancel, flush, and issue against the final. History, visible tools, and retrieved snippets that change during the wait also invalidate the buffer. Do not fire on every interim partial—partial jitter becomes a request storm. A common policy is to start only on an ASR final segment or a sufficiently stable partial. Tools with side effects must not execute preemptively: the model may propose a call; placing an order or transferring money waits for turn confirmation and the orchestrator. You can also presynthesize the first TTS sentence, but a miss wastes both LLM and TTS, so many systems preempt only the LLM. Three pauses in one utterance can mean three discarded requests; bill against both “hit rate × milliseconds saved” and “discarded-token share.”

**Follow-ups:** How you state the difference from speculative decoding in one sentence; whether preemption that fills the GPU at high concurrency actually raises everyone’s TTFT; if the final transcript only added a particle, whether you compare after normalization or require exact string equality.

**Extended questions:**
- Why short commands and booking flows are good defaults to leave on, and why dictating a long email or telling a story should turn it off?
- May a read-only lookup ride along with the preemptive request, and how do you design a staging area so writes cannot commit a dirty order?
- When the user pauses three times in one utterance, how do you estimate discarded-token cost and cap concurrency so the shared inference pool does not stall?

**Reasoning:** Endpoint wait and TTFT have no data dependence, so they can overlap; correctness comes from “never play before confirm” plus an input fingerprint compare. Failure modes are interim jitter and tools with side effects. Accept on hit rate, milliseconds saved per turn, discarded-token share, and voice-to-voice p95.

## MM-032. The agent jumps in after a half-second pause while the user is reading a card number, then on the next call it sits silent after a finished question. Where does a silence-only rule fail, and where does adding a “this utterance is semantically complete” score fail?

**Answer:** Turn-taking continuously answers three questions: has the user finished, is it my turn to speak, and are they speaking again while I speak. A silence-only threshold treats “thinking pause inside a card number” and “gap after a finished question” as the same acoustic event, so a short threshold cuts digits in half and a long one leaves a closed question hanging. Semantic end-of-turn reads the current transcript (sometimes with energy or pitch) and scores “this is already actionable”: unfinished card numbers, addresses, and enumerations stay low and you keep waiting; “what time do you open” scores high and you may answer after a very short silence. Production is a cascade: VAD finds a gap, the semantic score decides whether to keep waiting—not an either/or.

**Deep dive:** VAD only answers whether speech energy is present; it knows nothing about content. Phone noise, breaths, and keyboards make it jitter. A silence window of about 400–800 ms is the usual sketch: short cuts in, long dead-air. The semantic head should learn syntactic/intent completeness, not redo ASR; it will false-complete when ASR hears “do you open” as a question particle, and it will score systematically low when spoken Chinese drops the final particle. Backchannels (“mm”, “uh-huh”) are not a new turn—require a minimum speech duration or filter fillers, or the agent treats acknowledgements as interruptions. Half-duplex pipelines must make this decision explicit; full-duplex models fold listen and speak together and hide turn-taking in implicit behavior that is harder to log. For known-length readouts (card numbers, ID numbers), add a rule prior (do not end before the digit count) rather than handing the decision entirely to a score. On noisy lines, relax VAD sensitivity and the silence window together and trade a little delay for fewer cut-ins. This question only decides “has the user finished”; it does not treat the acoustic echo problem of the user speaking over the agent.

**Follow-ups:** Whether spoken Chinese, which often drops final particles, makes semantic scores systematically low and needs a per-language threshold; which ships first, the silence window or the semantic head, and how you gate them on cut-in rate versus dead-air rate; after a false cut, how you roll back the half sentence already spoken and the dialogue history.

**Extended questions:**
- How do you label “safe to answer / keep waiting” offline so you do not train on silence duration itself and learn the old error back?
- Does a fixed ~700 ms window create more cut-ins or more dead air in cabin noise, and can the semantic head fix that alone?
- When a 16-digit card number length is known, who wins—the rule or the semantic model—and what if the digits are complete but the user is still repeating a check digit?

**Reasoning:** Split “finished” into an acoustic gap and semantic completeness. The two product failures—cutting a card number and stalling after a question—prove one signal is not enough. The combination gives two knobs. Accept on false-cut rate, missed-end rate, and a dedicated set of digit-string and address turns.

## MM-033. On speakerphone the agent cuts itself off mid-sentence, and a cough also stops playback—how do you set echo cancellation and the barge-in confirm window so a real interruption stops in a beat, while echo and “mm-hmm” do not?

**Answer:** Self-interruption is the loudspeaker leaking into the microphone so VAD treats the agent as the user. Run acoustic echo cancellation first: estimate the echo path from the reference being played, subtract it from the microphone, then run VAD. Do not stop on any voice activity: require a minimum speech duration (for example about 300–500 ms) or two or three words from a continuously running STT; coughs, backchannels, and residual echo usually fail that gate. After confirm, stop speaker → playback buffer → TTS → LLM in that order, and write into history only the words the user actually heard, because the LLM emits text far faster than audio plays.

**Deep dive:** The AEC reference must be the path that actually reached the DAC, including resampling and the volume curve, or the estimator cannot lock. Speakerphone, car, and laptop speakers leave an echo tail of tens to a couple of hundred milliseconds; the confirm window must be longer than that tail or a residual peak looks like barge-in. Policies from fast to stable: energy gate (fast, many false stops) → minimum duration → word-count gate (needs a streaming STT that is always on). Noisy phone lines need a longer window; a headset or close-talk mic can go shorter. Stopping in the wrong order leaves a ghost: the LLM is dead but 200–400 ms of audio is still in the buffer. Align history by mapping playback progress back to text (character or phoneme timestamps) and trim the unplayed half-sentence from the assistant message, or the next turn will quote a price the user never heard. For speed, duck playback gain near zero first and tear the tasks down only after confirm; if STT labels a backchannel, restore gain and the user barely hears a hitch. Even with a full-duplex speech model you still need a “user speech vs local playback” decision; do not assume the model can tell them apart.

**Follow-ups:** How do you test AEC by looping the reference back, and at what residual level is it safe to tighten the confirm window; how do you align playback timestamps to TTS text at word granularity; if the user says “wait,” is that a topic change or a pause, and how does the state machine tell them apart.

**Extended questions:**
- What mix of echo, coughs, affirmative backchannels, and overlapping speech belongs in the eval set, and why do clean recordings undercount false stops?
- After barge-in, do you immediately say “go ahead” or wait half a second to confirm—which one hurts latency, and which one hurts politeness?
- When a small buffer cuts barge-in delay, how do you void both the TTS sentence queue and sentences not yet synthesized?

**Reasoning:** The goal is that a real interruption stops quickly and a false signal does not. The constraint is that echo and backchannels look like short speech on the waveform. Subtract self-audio with AEC, filter noise with a duration or word gate, then stop cleanly and keep history to what was heard. Accept on barge-in response time, false-barge rate, self-interrupt rate, and post-interrupt citation consistency.

## MM-034. The order-lookup API’s p95 is 2.8 seconds, and users say “hello?” twice into the silence—when should a filler phrase start, and should it be prerecorded or synthesized, so you cut perceived wait without overlapping the real answer?

**Answer:** A filler phrase cuts perceived wait, not wall-clock API time: if the user hears nothing for about 800–1200 ms after they stop talking, they assume the call dropped and barge in. For a tool you already know is slow (p95 above about 1 second), play a short acknowledgement the moment the call starts; for uncertain latency add a timer fallback—for example if no speakable first sentence is ready about 1 second after end-of-turn, play the next line from inventory. Prefer a prerecorded or presynthesized list in the same voice as the agent so you do not queue another TTS job; the moment the real answer’s first packet arrives, cut to the body, and let a half-played filler stop on a sentence boundary so the two streams do not overlap.

**Deep dive:** Illustrative timeline, not a product spec: within about 300 ms after endpointing you have the final transcript and the LLM has decided to look up the order, so you start “Let me check that order for you,” which lasts about 1.5–2 seconds; the tool runs in parallel, and at a 2.8 s p95 the filler is just ending, leaving a couple of hundred milliseconds of residual silence. Three audio sources: prerecorded is fastest and has no synthesis jitter, but the line cannot include an order id and must match the live TTS voice, sample rate, and loudness or the user hears a different person; a fixed list synthesized on the fly is easy to edit without rerecording, but each play adds tens to a couple of hundred milliseconds of TTS; letting the LLM emit a preamble before the tool call is the most on-topic (“I’ll look up 4521”), yet the first token itself costs hundreds of milliseconds and may be too late to be the first sound on a slow tool. Keep at least 4–6 lines and rotate them; never play the same line on two consecutive turns. The filler must be written into history as an assistant message, or the next LLM turn will not know it already said “let me check” and will say it again. If the user barges in mid-filler: stop playback, keep only the words that were actually heard, and cancel the tool if the new utterance changes the lookup target. Commitment wording (“I’ll place that order”) is riskier than lookup wording (“I’ll check”), because the tool can fail.

**Follow-ups:** Do you still play a filler on a 100 ms cache hit; if you move the timer threshold from 600 ms to 1500 ms, how do “hello?” barges trade off against “too much chatter” complaints; how do you crossfade the filler TTS stream into the real answer so two voices never overlap.

**Extended questions:**
- Why is a completed-state filler like “I’ve booked that for you” more dangerous than “I’ll look it up”?
- When the tool takes 10 seconds, how do you schedule progress lines after the first filler so it does not sound like a station announcement?
- What does the user hear if a prerecorded filler and live TTS disagree on sample rate or loudness, and how do you accept that?

**Reasoning:** The goal is to kill the “dropped call” feeling. The constraint is that tool wall-clock cannot be erased and the line cannot play two voices. Decide whether to speak from the tool’s p95, pick the audio source that gets the first sound out earliest, then use history writes and sentence-boundary cuts so you neither repeat nor overlap. Accept on time-to-first-audio, residual silence after the filler, and the rate of “hello?”, not on API latency alone.

## MM-035. The LLM is still writing sentence three, but the user must hear sentence one first. What boundaries should a chunker use when handing streamed tokens to TTS, and how do cutting the first chunk early versus waiting for a full sentence change latency and pronunciation?

**Answer:** If you wait for the whole reply before TTS, the silence the user hears is roughly full generation time plus TTS first packet. Sentence-level chunking sends the first closed sentence to synthesis immediately; later sentences generate while the first one plays, so the pipeline overlaps. The chunker buffers tokens and emits on punctuation or syntactic boundaries rather than on every token—TTS needs right context for pronunciation and tune. First-chunk policy should differ from later chunks: bias the first chunk early (comma or short clause) to cut time-to-first-audio; bias later chunks to full sentences for naturalness. Early cuts buy opening latency and pay with questions read as statements and polyphones resolved on the wrong continuation.

**Deep dive:** Timing sketch: TTFT 0.3 s, then about ten tokens per 0.1 s, TTS speaks 0.2 s after it receives a chunk, three sentences of about ten tokens each. Waiting for all three, time-to-speech is about 0.3+0.3+0.2 = 0.8 s and grows linearly as the reply lengthens; sending at the first sentence close is about “time to finish sentence one + 0.2 s.” Chinese cannot use “period plus space”: a full stop is often not followed by a space, so cut on 「。！？」 and clause words. “3.5”, “10 a.m.”, and “Dr.” need an abbreviation table so you do not cut on the dot. Strip Markdown stars and heading marks before TTS. On barge-in you must flush the unplayed queue or the old sentence keeps talking. A TTS that only accepts independent sentences must cut at sentence end; a streaming TTS that keeps cross-chunk context is what makes a smaller first chunk safe. Hard-cutting lists and “first, second” items makes them sound like three unrelated commands.

**Follow-ups:** How you disambiguate a decimal point from an English period in mixed Chinese–English; what minimum first-chunk word count avoids speaking “okay” as its own flat sentence; when the user cuts in, how the chunker buffer, the TTS queue, and the already-played text stay aligned.

**Extended questions:**
- If the first chunk cuts at the first comma and the rest of the sentence restarts at a new pitch, do you restore cross-chunk context or forbid comma cuts on the first chunk?
- Does sentence-cutting a list drop the “still enumerating” tune, and should the chunker detect list structure?
- How do you draw a tunable curve of mis-segment rate versus time-to-first-audio instead of one fixed punctuation rule for every language?

**Reasoning:** The bottleneck is the conflict between “wait for the whole generation” and “TTS needs right context,” so cut on sentences (and an earlier first chunk) to overlap synthesis with continued writing. Rules must follow the writing system; interruptions must flush the queue. Accept on first-packet latency, pronunciation/intonation errors from bad cuts, and leftover playback after an interruption.
